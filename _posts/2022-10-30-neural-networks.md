So far we discussed multiple points:
- Neuroscientis are studying the brain by taking a reductionist approach, studying systems and looking for universal princinples that are related to all brains.
- We are interested in the computation that is carried out by the brain.
- The rules that governs computation are universal and unrelated to the specifics of the biological implementation of the brain.
- Artificial neural networks might be a suitable architecure for mimicking this type of computation in a machine.

In this manner comparing biological neurons and artificial neurons is like comparing apples to oranges.

However, we have to recall that most of the experiments are done in neuroscience and not performed on humans, and in fact mice brains on the biological level are very different than humans. We managed to cure parkinson's on mice <ref> however we don't know how to do it on humans because mice brains and human brains are loosley related.
  
Being loosely related means that it is hard to predict apriory when the two objects will be similar and when they will be different. It can only offer a good experiment, one where both outcomes can teach us something. When the two are similar we will gain evidence for a property being unverisal or important, and when the two are different we will gain evidence for the property being not crucial for the brain.
  
An example for loosely related objects outside this discussion is a sum and an integral. Sometimes it can be beneficial to replace a sum of discerete objects with its continous counterpart, and vice versa. It is an experiment that can give us intuition towards the answer/property we are looking for. For example the harmonic-sum: Sum(1/n) behaves similarly to Integral(1/x) = ln x. both are diverging, but it is much simpler to observe in the continous case.
  
If we are able to come up with a theory describing when ANNs and BNNs are actually similar, we will be able to study the (properties of) brain in a much simpler and elegant manner. We hyptothesize that both biological and artficial neurons actually behave similary even though they are very different.
  
This is because the behavior of cells is largly dominated by the optmization process and the task this neurons are trying to solve (ref to ganguli). We can impose of the artificial network a problem which is similar to the problem the biological network are trying to solve - for example classifying cats/dogs. We impose this problem on a (different) hierachical processing structure, and run a (probably different) optimization algorithm. This algorithm will look for solution for that problem, and across they way it might come across useful representations, which are useful for this task. We have already observe this phenomenon across different ANNs, their neurons behave similarly.
 
Therefore we claim that comparing  biological neurons and artificial neurons is like comparing apples to planets. These very different objects are similar because there are similar forces that are applied upon them. Even though the early astronomers are observing starts, they do so to study the invisible forces that guides their movements, however apples which are much more tractable and easy to study, fall according to these very same rules. Analogously, if we can study the princinples (forces) of intelligence through ANNs (apples) rather than BNNs (planets) we should embrace that.
